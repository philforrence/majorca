Hollowing mainly cuts weight and heat buildup. Part B §5 explains the method.

### 4. Reference material and licensing (details in Part D)

- **Corrections to the project brief:**
  - The statue is on **MLK Drive**, just north of Black Road and across from the Humboldt statue.
  - Elkington & Co. cast it; **the sculptor is unknown**.

- **Licensing:**
  - The statue itself is **public domain**: you can model it, print it, cast it, display it and even sell it.
  - Other people's photos and scans carry their own licenses. Using them as private visual reference while modeling is fine. Photogrammetry from someone else's photos, or redistributing them, is not.
  - **Your own photos and scan are legally clean.** Fairmount Park's permits apply to professional or media shoots; nothing found requires one for personal photos.

### 5. Scams and buying tips for local pickup
- **Red flags:**
  - A P2S under about $350, or a P1S under about $200, with no hours or photos.
  - A seller who is "out of town" and offers shipping only.
  - Requests for Zelle, Cash App, wire, gift cards, or PayPal Friends & Family.
  - New accounts.
  - Stock photos.
  - Pressure to put down a deposit.
- **Known scam patterns:** Bambu lookalike websites (e.g. "bamboolab") and FB/Instagram ads offering "clearance" at up to 75% off. Also a fake Amazon seller ("Nicolas Electronics Trading LLC"). Buy new only from store.bambulab.com or the dealers listed at bambulab.com/dealer.
- **At pickup:**
  - Meet at a police-station safe exchange zone, or at the seller's home if the printer is set up there.
  - Ask for a **test print while you watch**.
  - On the touchscreen, check **print hours** (Settings, device info) and confirm the **firmware updates** work.
  - Make sure the seller has **unbound the printer from their Bambu account**, or that it is factory-reset. A printer still bound to someone else's account is a red flag.
  - Check the hotend and nozzle for clogs and wear, the plate for gouges, the carbon rods or rails for wear, and the camera.
  - If an AMS is included, run a filament swap test.
- **Ask what's included:** AMS or AMS 2 Pro, spare hotends or nozzles (0.4 and others), PEI plates, the original box, and the power cord.
- **Pay safely:** in cash in person after testing, or through eBay or Mercari checkout for shipped items (buyer protection). On eBay, also check seller feedback and that returns are accepted.
- **Pricing rule of thumb:** a used P1S should be clearly below the **$339 refurbished price with a 2-yr warranty**. A used P2S should be clearly below **$549 new** (target $450 or less), or wait for the Black Friday announcement around late October.

### 3. Material quantity math

#### Assumptions (stated so they can be changed)
- 10" St. George solid volume: **450 cm³** (midpoint of the 300–600 cm³ brief). 4" piece: 450 × 0.4³ = 450 × 0.064 = **29 cm³** (range 19–38 cm³).
- Surface area. A sphere of 450 cm³ has r = 4.75 cm and SA = 284 cm². A horse, rider and dragon with legs, lance and wings has about 3–4× the area of that sphere, so assume **SA about 1,000 cm² (155 in²)** for the 10". The 4" has 1,000 × 0.4² = **160 cm² (25 in²)**.
- Densities (typical Smooth-On TDS values from memory; check the technical bulletins): platinum silicone SG about 1.08–1.2, i.e. **about 23 in³/lb** for Mold Star and Rebound (Dragon Skin is about 25.7 in³/lb). Urethane resin SG about 1.05. Bronze about 8.8 g/cm³.
- Cold-cast mix: **1 part resin : 2 parts bronze by weight**. Smooth-On's cold-casting guidance runs from roughly 1:1 up to about 1:3. Per 100 g resin (95 cm³) + 200 g bronze (22.7 cm³), you get 117.7 cm³ weighing 300 g, so **2.55 g/cm³**. The mix is 1/3 resin and 2/3 bronze by mass.

#### 3a. Silicone

**4" test, poured block mold (Mold Star 15 SLOW):**
- Bounding box about 4 × 3.2 × 1.6 in. With 0.5" walls: 5 × 4.2 × 2.6 = 54.6 in³, minus the model (1.8 in³), leaves 53 in³. At 23 in³/lb that is **2.3 lb**, so one trial (2 lb) is short.
- With 3/8" walls (or a box shaped closer to the model): 4.75 × 3.95 × 2.35 = 44 in³, minus 1.8, leaves 42 in³ = **1.8 lb**. **One 2-lb trial fits.** Budget a second trial (+$35.66) if the shape is awkward.
- A 4" brush-on mold would need only 25 in² + flanges (≈40 in²) × 0.25" = 10 in³ = **0.45 lb**, but it also needs a mother mold. A block pour is simpler for the test.

**10" St. George, block mold for comparison:**
- Bounding box about 10 × 8 × 4 in. With 0.5" walls: 11 × 9 × 5 = 495 in³, minus 27.5 in³ (the model), leaves **467 in³ = about 20 lb**. Shaping the box closer to the model (filling corners with clay or foam) might cut that to about 12 lb.
- That means a Mold Star 15 gallon unit (about 16 lb, $226.28) plus a trial, or about **$226–$300 in silicone**. Cutting a two-part block apart around the lance, legs and wings is also hard. **Not recommended.**

**10" St. George, brush-on glove mold (Rebound 25), recommended:**
- Area 155 in² + parting flanges and keys (about 45 in²) = 200 in². At 1/4" total thickness (first coat thin, then 2–3 coats thickened with Thi-Vex), that is 200 × 0.25 = **50 in³ = 2.2 lb**.
- With about 30% waste in cups and brushes, that rises to **2.8 lb**. **Buy 2 trial units (4 lb) = $76.72.** The gallon ($247.58) only makes sense if you will make several molds.
- Thi-Vex: about 1% of the rubber weight on the build coats, which is about 18 g, so the 1 oz bottle ($4.21) is enough.

**Mother mold for the 10" (two-piece shell):**
- Shell area about 250 in² at 1/4–3/8" thick, so roughly 60–95 in³.
- Plasti-Paste II: about 2 trials ($100.30); its coverage per trial is my estimate. Alternative: plaster bandage, about 3 rolls × $19.24 = $57.72.

### 5. Hollow casting: slush and rotational

**What it is:** Instead of filling the mold, you coat its inside with resin in thin layers until a shell about 3–6 mm thick has built up. **Slush casting** means pouring resin in, sloshing it around, and pouring the excess out, repeated several times. **Rotational (roto) casting** means sealing a measured amount of resin inside a closed mold and turning it in every direction, by hand or on a roto-caster, until it gels evenly on all walls.

**Cold-bronze process, step by step:**
1. **Prep the mold.** Use a light coat of Ease Release 200 and let it flash off. Many casters skip release on platinum silicone with urethane for the first few pulls. Dust the dry mold with bronze powder using a soft brush, then tap out the loose excess. This gives the most metallic surface.
2. **Face coat.** Mix a small batch of slow resin (**Smooth-Cast 326**, 9-min pot life). Mix A and B first, then stir in bronze powder at about **1:1 to 1:2 resin:bronze by weight**, until it is the consistency of thick cream. Brush it into every detail (horse's mane, scales, armor) and rotate the mold so it coats evenly, aiming for **1–2 mm**. Pop bubbles with the brush. Let it gel until tacky.
3. **Second face coat** (optional) to cover any thin spots.
4. **Close the mold** in its mother mold, band it, and seal the pour spout.
5. **Backing layers.** Pour in a measured shot of fast resin (**Smooth-Cast 325** or **65D**, which gels on the wall), roughly 1/4–1/3 of the 250 cm³ backing total per shot. Rotate continuously in all axes until it gels (about 2–3 min). Repeat 2–3 times until the wall reaches **3–6 mm** (about 3 mm for a 4" piece, about 4–6 mm for a 10" piece). Tint 325 with a little bronze-brown pigment so chips don't show white.
6. **Optional backfill for weight or strength.** Pour plain resin, or resin filled with a cheap powder (e.g., calcium carbonate, sand or glass microballoons; not priced here), into the base only. This adds bottom weight for stability. Expanding urethane foam can fill thin, weak areas such as horse legs. Glue a threaded insert or steel rod into the base if the piece will be mounted.
7. **Demold** after the full cure (325: 10 min to handle, about 4 hr for full properties; 326: 60 min). Trim the seams.
8. **Finish.** Burnish with #0000 steel wool or Scotch-Brite 7448 to expose metal on the high points. Optionally darken with Sculpt Nouveau black patina or liver of sulfur, which only react on exposed metal particles, so test on the 4". Or rub in black shoe polish or black acrylic and wipe back so it stays in the recesses. Buff again, then seal with Renaissance Wax or Sculpt Nouveau metal wax. Rub 'n Buff can add gold accents.

**Typical wall thickness:** 3–6 mm overall (1–2 mm bronze face plus 2–4 mm resin backing).

**Material savings (see the table in 3b):** On this 10" design, hollowing saves about 5–15% of resin vs a solid pour with a face coat, because much of the volume is thin limbs. It saves about 0.9–1.1 lb of bronze (about $35–50) vs a fully loaded solid pour. On a chunkier piece (a bust or a solid animal) a hollow cast can use 50–70% less resin.

**Pros:** lighter (about 650 g vs 1,150 g fully loaded); less bronze; less heat when curing (thick urethane pours can run hot and shrink or warp); finishes like a fully loaded cast because only the skin is visible.

**Cons:** more labor and timing skill; risk of thin spots or pinholes, especially on lance tips and horse legs (solid-fill those); the piece can feel too light, so backfill the base; a hollow cast sounds "plasticky" when tapped; it needs a closed, sealable mold (the mother mold must band tightly).

### 6. Safety notes

- **Urethane resins (Smooth-Cast/EasyFlo)** contain isocyanates in Part A. Work with cross-ventilation or an exhaust fan and wear an **OV respirator (3M 6200 + 60921)** while mixing large batches. Wear nitrile gloves and eye protection. Smooth-On shop listings say "For Industrial Use Only", so read each SDS.
- **Bronze powder** is a fine metal dust. Wear the **P100** part of the cartridge while measuring or dusting, and don't sweep it dry. Keep it away from sparks and open flame, since fine metal dust can burn. Store it sealed.
- **Platinum silicone is cure-inhibited by sulfur** (sulfur-based clays like Roma Plastilina, latex gloves, some tapes and primers, fresh paint). Use **sulfur-free clay and nitrile gloves**. Let filler primer fully cure for 24 h or more, and **do a small test patch** of silicone on your primed PLA before pouring the real mold.
- **Liver of sulfur** releases hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg smell). Use it outdoors or with ventilation, and keep it away from your silicone molds and uncured silicone.
- **Sanding Bondo and filler primer:** wear a P100 or N95 mask. Wet-sand where possible.
- **Steel wool** sheds fragments, so wipe the piece before waxing.
- **Pressure pot (optional):** it is a converted paint tank. Never exceed its 60 psi rating. Use the safety valve and replace the gasket as needed.
- Keep the patina chemicals and resins away from children and pets. Dispose of liquid A and B only after mixing and curing them.

### Practical notes pulled from the sources
- PLA masters: sand to at least 800 grit (Hubs and Standard Print Co), or coat with XTC-3D. Don't power-sand PLA, because the heat softens it.
- Test platinum silicone against your primer or filler for cure inhibition, or use tin-cure silicone (Instructables report).
- Bronze powder: -325 mesh. Common loadings are 1:1 by volume up to about 200–300% of resin weight (Silpak). Tint the resin dark brown or black.
- Gel coat, wait 5–10 minutes until partly cured, then backfill. Don't let it fully cure or the layers can delaminate (Smooth-On).
- Burnish with #0000 steel wool. Dusting-method casts take chemical patina best (Polytek). Seal with wax or acrylic.

**Best references: take your own photos.** The statue sits at ground level beside a public trail, so you can walk all the way around it.

**Photogrammetry workflow (phone):**
1. Go on an **overcast day**. Bronze is shiny, and soft light reduces glare. Morning avoids crowds.
2. Shoot **150–250 photos**. Make 3 rings around the statue at different heights: low, waist, and as high as you can reach with a monopod or selfie pole. Overlap shots by about 70%. Add close-up passes on the rider's head, the horse's head, the dragon's head and claws, the lance and the underside of the horse.
3. Lock exposure and focus. Shoot RAW or high-quality JPEG. Don't shoot into the sun.
4. The base (6 ft) puts the group at about 6–14 ft. A **phone on a 2–3 m pole** (interval timer or Bluetooth trigger) gets the top surfaces. **Drones are restricted in Fairmount Park**, so check the rules before flying one.
5. Process in **RealityScan** (free mobile and desktop, Epic), **Polycam**, **Meshroom** (free, open source), or Agisoft Metashape.
6. Clean up and retopologize in Blender. Scale the model so the statue (not the base) is **8 ft tall**, then shrink to your 10" target. Close holes under the horse and dragon by hand-sculpting.
7. Note any thin parts for the print and mold: the **lance**, the reins, the dragon's wings and tail, and the horse's legs. You may need to thicken them or add a hidden armature.

### 4. Licensing explainer (US; not legal advice)

- **The statue itself is in the public domain.** It was made in 1872–77, and any US or UK copyright has long expired. Anyone may model, copy, cast, display and **sell** replicas of the *design*. There is no copyright obstacle to a 10" cold-cast copy, on display or for sale.
- **Trademark and endorsement:** don't imply the replica is endorsed by the City, the Association for Public Art, or the St. George Society, and don't use their logos.
- **Photos of 3D objects:** a photo of a sculpture *can* carry the **photographer's copyright** (angle, lighting and framing are creative choices). *Bridgeman v. Corel* (S.D.N.Y. 1999) covers only faithful photos of **2D** public-domain works. So APA, philart, Google and Flickr photos stay the photographers' property unless they carry a CC or PD license.
  - **Using them as visual reference** to sculpt your own model (no copying of the image into the product) is ordinary artistic practice. The sculpture you produce copies the public-domain statue, not the photo.
  - **Projecting or tracing a photo directly onto a texture, or republishing the photos**, needs a license. CC-BY or CC-BY-SA requires credit; NC bars commercial use.
  - Using copyrighted photos as **photogrammetry input** produces a derivative of those photos, which is legally murkier. Use only your own photos, or CC0 / CC-BY photos, for photogrammetry.
- **3D scans of public-domain objects:** *Meshwerks v. Toyota* (10th Cir. 2008) held that a faithful digital wireframe of an existing object lacked originality and was not copyrightable. Commentators read it as suggesting that pure scans of public-domain sculptures carry little or no copyright. It is one circuit, a car not a sculpture, and is not settled. **Website licenses are contracts.** If you download a scan under CC BY-NC or "personal use only," you have agreed to those terms. Respect them, whatever the copyright status.
- **What each license allows:**
  - **CC0 / PD:** anything, including selling.
  - **CC BY:** anything, with credit.
  - **CC BY-SA:** anything, with credit; derived models must use the same license (physical casts are generally fine to sell).
  - **CC BY-NC:** print, cast and display for non-commercial purposes only; **no selling** of files or casts.
  - **"Personal use only" / standard marketplace license:** usually no selling of prints or casts and no molds for sale; check each EULA.
- **Cleanest route if you might sell or display commercially:** use **your own photogrammetry** of the public-domain statue, plus your own sculpting. Then you own everything.

### 5. Recommended approach for Blender

1. **Best option: your own photogrammetry** (workflow in §2). It is the only route to an accurate likeness of *this* statue, since no scan exists online, and it is legally clean. Expect a good scan of the horse and dragon bodies. The lance, reins, tail and undercuts need hand repair.
2. **Fallback: sculpt from reference.** Gather Commons images (after checking their licenses), APA and philart photos (private reference only) and Street View. Block out the group in Blender using the APA proportions: 8 ft high, 12'6" deep, 4 ft wide. For horse anatomy you could start from a **CC-BY or CC-BY-SA equestrian scan** (e.g. Friedrich der Große, CC BY-SA). Avoid NC models as a base if you might ever sell copies. Then repose and resculpt the horse, rider and dragon to match your photos.
3. **Print/mold prep at 10" (scale ≈ 1:9.6 for the statue alone, or ≈ 1:16.8 with the base):**
   - Thicken the lance to at least 2–3 mm, or model it as a separate brass-rod insert.
   - Merge the horse's legs, dragon and base into a solid footprint.
   - Make the piece hollow or solid depending on the casting resin.
   - Plan a 2-part or block silicone mold with the undercut beneath the horse in mind. It is often easier to split the model: horse plus rider, dragon plus base, and the lance separately.
4. **Display text:** use "Cast by Elkington & Co., Birmingham, 1877; commissioned by the Society of the Sons of St. George; sculptor unknown; West Fairmount Park, MLK Drive at Black Road." **Do not claim it was shown at the 1876 Centennial** unless a primary source turns up.
